Walk through a satellite
instrument's brain

EDDI-FPGA is the digital signal processor for a small space instrument with two jobs: listen to the ionosphere — the shell of electrified gas around Earth — and feel space debris hitting the spacecraft. The original flew on a CubeSat in 2024 on a microcontroller that could only do one job at a time. This page walks through the Verilog that moves both jobs into an FPGA, in the order the signal experiences them.

Step 0

The big picture

One antenna signal, two completely different questions asked of it:

The whole reason this project exists: the flight microcontroller could only run one of those two paths at a time. On the FPGA they're separate pieces of physical hardware, so they run simultaneously. Keep that in mind — everything below is hardware being described, not a program executing line by line. Every always @(posedge clk) block in every module fires on the same clock tick, all at once, forever.

Step 1

The number system everything depends on

There's no floating point anywhere. Every value is an integer that we agree to interpret as a fraction. The convention is Q1.15: a 16-bit signed integer where we pretend the value is divided by 215 = 32768.

Multiplying two Q1.15 numbers gives a Q2.30 result (32 bits), so after every multiply you'll see >>> 15 — an arithmetic right shift that divides by 215 and puts the decimal point back where it belongs:

That's the entire trick. Whenever a shift follows a multiply in this code, it isn't magic — it's bookkeeping.

Step 2 · rtl/qtn_core.v

The front door of the ionosphere path

Why a window at all?

The FFT is going to chop the ADC stream into 1024-sample frames, and it mathematically assumes each frame repeats forever, end-to-start. If a frame ends at a different level than it began, that seam looks like a violent step edge — and a step edge contains every frequency, smearing garbage across the whole spectrum. The fix: multiply each frame by a bell curve (the Hann window) that fades both ends to zero. No ends, no seam.

The heart of the machine

This module turns 1024 time samples into 1024 frequency bins, in three phases: load, compute, unload.

Load — the bit-reversal shuffle

The entire FFT is one small operation repeated 5,120 times. Take two values A and B out of RAM, multiply B by a twiddle factor W (a point on the unit circle — the rotating ruler the FFT measures against), and put back their sum and difference:

Two safety details inside the math

Scaling. Adding two numbers can double the magnitude; do that ten rounds in a row and you overflow 16 bits by a factor of 1024. So every butterfly divides its results by 2 — after ten rounds the output equals FFT/1024, which conveniently matches NumPy's fft(x)/N, making verification a direct comparison.

Step 4 · rtl/psd_avg.v

From spectrum to measurement

The FFT streams out 513 complex bins (for real input the top half of the spectrum is a mirror image, so bins 0…512 carry everything). Each becomes a power: real² + imaginary². Phase is thrown away — for this measurement only energy matters. And because one spectrum of thermal hiss is too jittery to read, powers are accumulated across several frames and averaged. Noise is random and cancels; the plasma peak is not, and stays.

Step 5 · rtl/debris_detector.v

Statistics with a hair trigger

Different philosophy entirely. The QTN path processes everything identically, forever. The debris path must judge — almost everything it sees is boring, and it has to catch the one-in-a-million exception without crying wolf. Its life is a five-state loop:

The trigger: 4σ without a square root

Calibration learns what "normal" looks like: the average level, the noise spread σ, and how often the signal wobbles across its own average. Then a sample is suspicious if it sits more than 4 standard deviations out — a one-in-16,000 fluke for noise. But computing σ needs a square root, which is genuinely painful in hardware. So the code squares both sides of the inequality instead:

Qualification: the fingerprint check

Loudness alone isn't proof — interference is loud too. But an impact has a signature: the vaporised dust grain's plasma cloud rings at kilohertz, far slower than the fizzy background. So for the first 128 samples after a trigger, the detector counts mean-crossings and demands the event wobble at less than a third of the background rate:

The capture buffer: recording the past

The best part. The buffer is circular — 1536 slots, always recording, new samples overwriting the oldest:

By the time the trigger fires, the 512 samples of "before" already sit in the buffer; the detector just stops overwriting at the right moment. The snapshot then holds in READY until the spacecraft computer collects it — or, per another review fix, until a timeout auto-releases it and bumps an events_dropped counter, because a detector frozen waiting on a dead comms link would miss every subsequent impact for the rest of the mission.

What the adversarial review changed

Passing tests only proves the code handles what the tests imagined. An independent review pass hunted for what they didn't — degenerate inputs, stuck links, orbital drift — and reproduced two real bugs in simulation. All confirmed findings are fixed and re-verified; they're marked where they appear in the walkthrough above.

The meta-lesson worth retelling: the original instrument's sister satellite was killed a month into its mission by a radiation event. For space hardware, the happy path is the easy 80% — robustness against the input you never imagined is the design problem.
